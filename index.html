2. American Literary Epochs & Timeline Movement

MN 11.1.5.1
  • Chronological Progression: Native Orality (Pre-1600) → Colonial / Puritan (1620–1750) → Revolutionary / Enlightenment (1750–1800) → Transcendentalism (1830–1865) → Realism → Modernism.
  • Historical Shifts: Literature reflects changing cultural values, technology, political struggles, and changing views of nature.
  • Continuity & Change: Early creation themes evolve into later national identity debates across each era.
📍 Historical Context Spotlight

"We do not study literary periods in isolation. Every era reacts against the ideas of the period that came before it!"

3. The Art of "Slow Looking" with Bob Ross

MN 11.1.8.1
  • Observational Methodology: Before analyzing complex text, practice patient observation using visual art.
  • The Bob Ross Exercise: Spend 3 minutes silently observing a painting or passage without judging or interpreting immediately.
  • Key Questions: What small details do you notice first? How do color, tone, and layering build a complete atmosphere?
  • Textual Application: Apply "Slow Looking" to complex sentences—notice word choices, cadence, and subtle imagery.
🎨 "Happy Accidents" in Close Reading

"In literature, as in painting, small background details are placed intentionally. Slow down and look closely before forming your thesis!"

4. Native American Oral Tradition & Cosmology

MN 11.1.6.1
  • Living Narratives: Storytelling is an active social and spiritual institution, preserving history, ethics, and ecological wisdom.
  • Environmental Cosmology: Humans are viewed as part of a sacred web of nature, rather than masters over creation.
  • Oral Mechanics: Repetition, rhythm, and audience participation reinforce communal memory across generations.
🌿 Key Concept: Orality vs. Literacy

"Oral literature is dynamic and performative, meant to adapt to the community's needs while maintaining sacred truths."

5. Creation Myths & The Earth-Diver Motif

MN 11.1.5.2
  • Universal Motifs: The "Earth-Diver" story type features a flood, a creator figure, and brave animals diving deep into primeval waters.
  • Sacrifice & Community: Small, humble creatures often succeed where larger, stronger animals fail.
  • Ecological Balance: Creation is framed as a collaborative act between spirit figures and the natural world.
💡 Motif Analysis

"Pay attention to which animal brings up the mud from the deep—their small size emphasizes perseverance over brute strength."

6. Deep Analysis: "Manabozho and the Muskrat"

MN 11.1.4.1
  • Focus Text: Ojibwe Creation Story featuring Manabozho (the cultural hero/trickster) and the Muskrat (Waizhask).
  • Narrative Arc: After a great flood, larger animals attempt to dive for earth and collapse exhausted. The humble muskrat steps forward.
  • Textual Moment: The muskrat risks its life to bring back a small grain of sand under its claws, enabling the restoration of the earth.
📖 Textual Passage Excerpt

"'I will try,' said the humble muskrat. He dived deep into the dark waters... when he floated up, in his tiny paw was the grain of earth that renewed the world."

7. The Trickster Archetype & Moral Instruction

MN 11.1.5.3
  • Character Dynamics: Trickster figures like Manabozho are complex—simultaneously wise, foolish, helpful, and impulse-driven.
  • Pedagogical Purpose: Stories use tricksters to teach community boundaries, humor, and respect for natural limits.
  • Symbolic Function: The trickster disrupts rigid orders to allow new creation and learning to emerge.
🎭 Archetype Breakdown

"Tricksters are never pure villains or pure heroes—they reflect human imperfection and adaptability."

8. Comparative Worldviews: Indigenous vs. Colonial

MN 11.1.6.2
  • Indigenous Worldview: Circular time, relational ecology, shared stewardship, oral preservation.
  • Early European Colonial Worldview: Linear time, wilderness to be subdued, individual ownership, written doctrine.
  • Analytical Goal: Contrast how these differing perspectives shape early American literary themes and conflicts.
⚖️ Contrast Analysis

"Notice how land is described in each story: as a living relative to be respected, or as a wilderness to be cultivated."

9. Close Reading Strategies & Annotation

MN 11.1.8.2
  • Step 1: First Pass: Read for general narrative flow and initial impressions (Slow Looking).
  • Step 2: Second Pass: Highlight recurring motifs, word choices, figurative language, and structural shifts.
  • Step 3: Marginal Notes: Write explicit questions and connection points directly in the margins or digital notes.
📝 Annotation Standard

"Highlighting without writing notes in the margin is incomplete! Always write WHY a phrase matters."

10. Crafting Evidence-Based Thesis Statements

MN 11.2.5.1
  • Formula: Specific Textual Claim + Analytical Reason + Broader Cultural Significance.
  • Avoid Summarizing: A thesis is an arguable claim, not a plot recap of what happens in the text.
  • Integrating Quotes: Embed short direct quotes seamlessly into your analytical sentences with accurate citations.
🎯 Strong vs. Weak Thesis Check

"Weak: 'Muskrat dives for mud in the story.' | Strong: 'By elevating the humble muskrat over stronger creatures, the Ojibwe creation narrative establishes humility as a vital ecological virtue.'"

11. Seminar Circle & Discussion Ethics

MN 11.3.1.2
  • Active Listening: Build directly upon peer observations before offering counterpoints.
  • Evidence Anchor: Refer back to specific line numbers and page passages during discussion.
  • Sympathetic Engagement: Respect diverse cultural traditions and approach historic texts with curiosity.
💬 Seminar Rule of Thumb

"Always state: 'On page X, the author writes...' before offering your interpretation."
